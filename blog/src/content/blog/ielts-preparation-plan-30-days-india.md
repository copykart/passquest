## Before You Start: The One Thing Most Indian Students Skip

Before you open a single practice paper, you need to know your starting point — not just your overall band from a mock test, but your exact sub-skill breakdown.

Most Indian IELTS candidates have a predictable profile:

- **Reading:** Strong. Indian academic English exposure means most candidates score 6.5–7.5 on Reading with moderate preparation.
- **Listening:** Moderate. Section 4 (academic monologue) is the consistent weak point — the format is unfamiliar and the topics are specialist.
- **Writing:** Variable. Grammar and vocabulary are often strong. Coherence and Cohesion — the way ideas connect and progress — is where most Indian candidates lose the most points without knowing why.
- **Speaking:** Variable. Fluency is often good. Hesitation patterns and filler words (um, uh, you know) cost Fluency scores. Pronunciation is rarely a significant issue for educated Indian English speakers.

Your 30-day plan should not treat all four skills equally. It should identify which of these patterns applies to you specifically and load the plan accordingly.

## The Three Techniques That Move Scores Fastest for Indian Students

### Technique 1: the Coherence and Cohesion fix for Writing Task 2

Coherence and Cohesion is weighted equally with Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range in Writing Task 2 — but it is the criterion most Indian candidates never specifically practise.

The problem is over-reliance on discourse markers: *Firstly, Secondly, Furthermore, Moreover, In conclusion.* This pattern signals a template response rather than a developed argument. Examiners recognise it and score it at Band 5–6.

**The fix:** after your next Task 2 draft, remove every Firstly, Secondly and Furthermore. Rewrite each transition using a pronoun, a synonym, or a clause that connects logically rather than just signals sequence. The first draft will feel wrong. That discomfort is the learning.

**PassQuest tracks this:** the gap map scores your Writing Task 2 sessions against Coherence and Cohesion separately. You will see the sub-skill score move within sessions.

### Technique 2: the trigger keyword method for Listening Section 4

Section 4 is a five-to-six minute academic monologue on a specialist topic. Most Indian candidates lose three to four marks here compared to Sections 1 and 2.

**The fix:** in the 30-second preview before Section 4 plays, read each question and identify the one keyword you will listen for — not the answer, the trigger. The word in the question that will appear just before the answer in the recording.

When trigger 1 appears in the audio, Question 1 is about to be answered. When trigger 2 appears, Question 2 is coming. You stop listening to understand the content and start listening for your sequence. The academic material becomes background. The triggers become your navigation.

### Technique 3: the Not Given rule for Reading True/False/Not Given

True/False/Not Given produces more errors per question type than any other format in IELTS Reading. The confusion is always between **False** and **Not Given**.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>The rule that resolves this</strong>
  <p>If you cannot find a specific sentence in the passage that directly contradicts the statement, it is <strong>Not Given</strong>, not <strong>False</strong>. False requires direct contradiction. Not Given means the passage is silent on the matter. Absence of information is never False — it is Not Given.</p>
</div>

Write this on a Post-it note. Stick it to your desk. Apply it to every single True/False/Not Given answer for the rest of your preparation.

## What Most IELTS Guides Do Not Tell You

### Your IELTS score has a lower bound set by your weakest skill

If your Speaking is Band 6.0 and everything else is Band 7.5, your overall band is 6.5 — not 7.0. The overall band is the average of all four skills, rounded to the nearest 0.5. One weak skill caps your ceiling regardless of how strong the others are.

This means the first priority is always: **eliminate the floor, not build the ceiling.** Your strongest skill needs no work. Your weakest skill needs everything.

### Practice papers give you a score — not a diagnosis

Cambridge IELTS practice papers are excellent for simulating exam conditions. They are not designed to tell you why you are getting answers wrong. A score of 6.5 on a Reading practice paper tells you nothing about whether it was True/False/Not Given, matching headings or sentence completion that cost you. Without that breakdown you cannot fix the right thing.
