## First: Understand What Is Actually Costing You

Before you open a single practice paper, you need to know your specific starting point — not a general impression, but a precise breakdown of which of the four skills and which sub-skills within each skill are holding your band below your target.

Most Nigerian IELTS candidates follow a predictable profile. Understanding where you likely sit helps you allocate your 30 days correctly.

**Reading:** Strong. Nigerians are typically high-volume readers — newspapers, social media, religious texts, professional documents. Most Nigerian candidates score 6.5–7.5 on Reading with relatively moderate preparation. This is usually not the gap.

**Listening:** Moderate. The challenge is not understanding the audio — it is the specific question formats and the academic register of Section 4. Nigerian candidates often miss marks not because they cannot hear but because they do not know where in the audio to listen for the answer.

**Writing:** This is where most Nigerian candidates lose the most marks without understanding why. Vocabulary — strong. Grammar — reasonable. Coherence and Cohesion — consistently Band 5–6, pulling the entire Writing score down. The specific reason is explained in detail below.

**Speaking:** Confident. Nigerians are naturally expressive speakers and typically perform well in Parts 1 and 3. The gap for most Nigerian candidates is Speaking Part 2 — the long turn — where the absence of a clear structure causes the response to drift and lose marks on Coherence.

## The Three Things Nigerian Candidates Need to Fix Most

### Fix 1: The Writing Task 2 Coherence Problem

This is the single biggest score gap for Nigerian IELTS candidates. Here is the precise mechanism.

Nigerian secondary and university education produces strong writers — expressive, confident, with a broad vocabulary. But it also produces one specific pattern that IELTS examiners score at Band 5–6 without exception:

**The structural template.**

*"There are many reasons why this is the case. Firstly, it is important to consider… Secondly, one must also note that… Furthermore, it should be emphasised… In conclusion, it is clear that…"*

This is organised. It is clear. And it is marked as formulaic by every experienced IELTS examiner. The Band 6 Coherence descriptor specifically describes "some under- and over-use" of cohesive devices — and the over-use of Firstly, Secondly, Furthermore is exactly what it is describing.

Band 7 Coherence requires cohesive devices used **flexibly** — pronouns, synonyms, subordinate clauses, logical sequencing — not just discourse markers that label structure.

**The fix:** For your next three Task 2 essays, ban Firstly, Secondly, Furthermore, Moreover and In conclusion entirely. Connect your paragraphs using logic — pronouns, synonyms, relative clauses. The first essay will feel strange. That discomfort is the learning.

After three essays without these words, reintroduce them selectively — one per paragraph maximum, varied, not repeated.

This is the same Coherence and Cohesion mechanism we break down criterion by criterion in [the Band 7 Writing Task 2 guide](/ielts-writing-task-2-band-7-india).

### Fix 2: The Speaking Part 2 Structure Gap

Nigerian candidates are naturally expressive speakers and usually perform well in Parts 1 and 3. Part 2 — the long turn — is where most Nigerian candidates lose marks. Not because they run out of things to say, but because they say many things without a clear structure, and the response drifts.

Band 7 Speaking Part 2 Coherence requires a clear overall progression — not just fluency.

**The fix:** Apply the IDEA framework to every Part 2 topic card:

- **I — Introduce:** One clear sentence naming the subject
- **D — Describe:** Specific sensory or contextual detail — not general description
- **E — Explain:** Why it matters, why it stands out, what it means to you
- **A — Add:** A connected thought, a contrast, a reflection that extends naturally

Four moves. Two minutes. One central idea developed deeply — not four ideas listed shallowly. The depth is what earns Band 7.

### Fix 3: The Listening Section 4 Trap

Section 4 is a five-to-six minute academic monologue — a lecture or talk on a specialist topic. It is the most difficult Listening section and the one Nigerian candidates most consistently underperform on.

The difficulty is not vocabulary or comprehension. It is sustaining focus on a single academic thread for six minutes without any of the natural pauses or topic shifts that Sections 1–3 provide.

**The fix:** The keyword trigger technique. In the 30 seconds before Section 4 plays:

1. Read each question quickly
2. Identify the single keyword in each question that will appear just before the answer in the audio — not the answer itself, the trigger that signals the answer is coming
3. Write that keyword in the margin if you have time

During the recording, you are no longer listening to understand the lecture. You are listening for your sequence of triggers. When Trigger 1 appears — answer Question 1. When Trigger 2 appears — answer Question 2. The academic content is background. Your keyword sequence is the guide.

This technique typically moves Section 4 scores by 2–4 marks — which is the difference between Band 7.0 and Band 7.5 on Listening for many candidates.

## What Most Nigerian IELTS Guides Get Wrong

### Wrong advice 1: "Read English newspapers every day"

Reading English newspapers builds background vocabulary over months and years. It does not improve your IELTS Reading score in 30 days. IELTS Reading is a specific skill — skimming for main idea, scanning for specific information, inferring meaning — that requires practice with IELTS-format passages and IELTS question types. Reading the Guardian or Punch every morning is not preparation. It is background noise.

### Wrong advice 2: "Watch BBC to improve your Listening"

BBC English is not IELTS English. IELTS Listening uses a range of accents — British, Australian, North American, and others. It uses specific question formats — form completion, multiple choice, matching, map labelling — that have no equivalent in BBC programming. Watching BBC builds general listening comfort. It does not prepare you for Section 4 of the IELTS Listening test.

### Wrong advice 3: "Write an essay every day"

Writing one essay per day for 30 days produces 30 essays. If you do not know which of the four criteria each essay is failing on, you produce 30 essays at the same band as the first one. Volume without feedback is practice without improvement. Know your criterion-level score before and after each essay.
